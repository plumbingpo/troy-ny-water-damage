Attic Water Damage: What Troy Homeowners Should Know

Attic water damage comes from two directions. Rain gets in from above through worn shingles, failed flashing, or the seals around vents and chimneys. The house itself sends moisture upward too, and in a poorly ventilated attic it condenses on cold sheathing. Either way, hardly anyone goes up there to look. Our team in Troy inspects the attic, finds the source, and dries it out.

Wet insulation is the heart of most attic jobs. Heating and cooling bills go up because cellulose and fiberglass alike give up R-value when wet. Even worse, it keeps water pressed against the framing and the ceiling drywall for weeks. Our process is to pull the saturated insulation, dry the wood using dehumidifiers and air movers, and treat mold found on rafters or sheathing.

What It Looks Like

  • Dark stains or streaks on the rafters and the underside of the roof deck
  • An upstairs ceiling where the paint has blistered or started to peel
  • Flattened, matted or discolored insulation that stands out from what surrounds it
  • Light from outside visible along a roof valley or around a chimney or vent pipe
  • After rain, a stain or brown ring on a top floor ceiling that looks worse
  • Sheathing with fuzzy growth, black or white, concentrated toward the eaves

If Nothing Is Done

What Happens if You Wait

The first storm

Insulation Soaks It Up

Rain that makes it through the roof falls into the insulation and is absorbed. Nothing shows indoors, so the leak goes unnoticed and the roof is not repaired.

Hours 24 to 48

Mold Can Start

Damp wood and paper facing in a warm, still attic are ideal for mold. Growth can begin on the sheathing and on the back of the ceiling drywall.

Weeks later

Stains Come Through

Once storm after storm has saturated the insulation, the ceiling starts getting wet. The drywall may sag, and rings of brown staining appear on the ceiling of the room below.

Months later

Wood Starts Rotting

The sheathing comes apart in layers and no longer grips the roofing nails. Large areas of roof decking are covered in mold, while rafters that never dry begin to decay.

Our Process

What Happens After You Call

1

Inspect and Trace

To learn whether you have a leak, condensation or the two together, we go over the roof deck, the vents, the exhaust ducts and each flashing point. Its full reach is mapped with a thermal camera and meter readings.

2

Take Out Wet Insulation

Insulation that is soaked or has mold gets bagged and carried out, giving the wood and the drywall below a chance to dry. Rooms under the attic are protected, and the route in and out stays contained.

3

Dry the Wood

Air movers and dehumidifiers bring the rafters, joists and sheathing back to normal moisture levels. Visible mold is cleaned from the wood and an antimicrobial is applied.

4

Fix the Cause

The roof or ventilation issue behind it is pointed out to you, and we are glad to pass what we found to your roofer. Once the attic stays dry, new insulation goes in to restore the R-value.

What You Cannot See

The Damage You Do Not See

Insulation That Stops Working

Compressed, damp insulation does very little insulating, and it may not recover after it dries. The rooms beneath that section get colder or hotter, and you pay more for energy.

A Falling Ceiling

Insulation full of water weighs a lot. Wet from above, drywall loses strength at its fasteners and may fall without warning, with the wet insulation coming down too.

Leaks From Attic Equipment

In a lot of homes the attic houses an air handler along with its condensate line and pan. For weeks an overflowing pan or blocked drain can drip into insulation, and there is no storm to tip you off.

Bath Fans Venting Inside

Some bathroom fans exhaust into the attic and never reach the outdoors, adding warm, damp air daily. On cold sheathing that air condenses, and the result is easy to mistake for a leaking roof.
