Dealing With Appliance Leak Water Damage in Troy

Most of the appliance leaks we see trace back to three machines. Washer supply hoses can burst and keep spraying at full pressure until the valve is closed. A dishwasher tends to leak slowly from a door seal or drain hose, out of sight under the cabinets. A refrigerator ice maker line is a thin tube that can drip behind the unit for months. No two of these call for the same cleanup.

For homeowners in Troy, we begin by sliding the machine out to see how far the water traveled. For a kitchen, the usual places are below the flooring and under the cabinets. In laundry rooms it means the wall behind the machine and the rooms next door, or the ceiling below if the laundry is upstairs. We extract what is standing, measure what has soaked in, and set drying equipment sized to the wet area.

Do This First

Your First Steps

  1. Turn Off the ApplianceStop the cycle and unplug the machine if the outlet and floor around it are dry. Otherwise, use the breaker to cut its power.
  2. Shut Off the Water LineWashers have hot and cold valves on the wall behind them. Look below the sink for the valve that feeds a dishwasher. Refrigerator lines have a small valve behind or below.
  3. Soak Up Standing WaterGetting reachable water up with a mop or towels stops it from moving under the cabinets and the floor. Do not vacuum unless the machine is rated for wet and dry use.
  4. Keep the Door ClosedA dishwasher or washer that still holds water should stay closed. The entire load of water may spill across the floor if the door is opened mid-cycle.
  5. Photograph the Failed PartTake pictures of the hose, valve or fitting that failed and keep the part. Both the appliance maker and your insurer may want to look at it.

The appliance is disconnected and moved, and the supply is confirmed off. Then thermal imaging and moisture meters show us where water went below flooring, under the cabinets and into the walls nearby.

The Usual Culprits

Why This Happens

  • A clogged defrost drain or cracked drain pan that lets a refrigerator leak onto the floor.
  • A dishwasher with a failed pump seal, a cracked drain hose or a worn door gasket.
  • A kinked or cracked ice maker line, or one whose fitting has worked loose.
  • Overloading the drum or adding excess detergent, either of which can make the machine overflow.
  • A failed water inlet valve that keeps filling the washer or dishwasher after it should stop.
Why Speed Matters

What Happens if You Wait

Baseboards and Wall Bottoms

On a flooded kitchen or laundry floor, the baseboards and the lower edge of the drywall draw water upward. Often the worst of it is on the wall in back of the appliance, where it went unseen.

Kitchen Cabinets

Toe kicks and cabinet sides take on water from underneath when a dishwasher leaks. Swelling and separation follow, and for weeks the soaked cabinet boxes may trap moisture against the wall.

Rooms Next Door

Water may pour from a washer hose for hours on end. That water moves beneath walls into bedrooms, closets and hallways, soaking pad and carpet far from the laundry room.

Ceiling Below an Upstairs Laundry

In a home with an upstairs laundry, leak water passes through the floor and pools on the ceiling underneath. The drywall stains and sags, and the insulation above it stays wet.

Insurance

What About Insurance?

Homeowners policies often cover sudden and accidental events, and a burst hose or a machine overflowing with no warning is that kind of event. What is typically paid is the water damage, while the failed part and the appliance are left out. Slow seepage from a dishwasher or ice maker line is a harder claim, because gradual leaks usually are not covered. How old the hose was and how it was maintained may also come up with some insurers. Before cleanup, take photos, save the failed part and read your policy.

Cost

What Affects the Cost

  • Any mold growth, because it must be dealt with ahead of drying.
  • Type of water, since gray discharge requires sanitizing and clean supply water does not.
  • The appliance involved, and if the leak was a slow seep or a fast flood.
  • The time that passed before anyone found the leak, which matters most for slow drips at the back of a unit.
